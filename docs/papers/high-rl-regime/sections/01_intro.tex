\section{Introduction}


% P1 (~130 words). The shift: frontier RL is no longer small next to pretraining.
\talk{
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Frontier RL has grown from a thin layer to roughly pretraining scale. No lab publishes the per-model
    RL / pretraining FLOP ratio, so every number is an outside estimate. Three kinds; keep their units apart:
  \item \textbf{Per-model, FLOPs (lead with these; comparable to our runs).} Epoch (grade C): early 2025 RL ``maybe
    1--10\% of pre-training''; 2024 o-series 1--30\%; Grok 4 10--100\%; GPT-5 10--200\%. Ord (Oct 2025, D): the big
    three have not ``quite reached'' parity but are ``not far off''. Dan's graded range: 0.01--0.1 (2024, C),
    0.05--0.2 (H1 2025, C), 0.3--1 (H2 2025, D), about 1--2 (2026, D; only 0.1--0.4 if its GPU-time inputs are read as

  \item Our EI runs (0.9--19$\times$, RL-only FLOPs; P3) are at or above every per-model estimate, and the top of the

  \item Most of what we believe about what RL does was measured in the small-RL regime: Yue et al.'s RL is a median

  \item Possible opener: ``Reinforcement learning is no longer a thin layer on top of pretraining.''
  \item Cite: \cite{yueDoesReinforcementLearning2025,guoDeepSeekR1IncentivizesReasoning2025}. Not in references.bib

\end{itemize}}
Over the past two years, post-training and RL at frontier labs have grown from a small fraction of training compute to
a share comparable with pretraining (Table~\ref{tab:compute-share}). Most of what we know about the effects of RL comes
from the earlier, low-RL regime. For instance, the RL in Yue et al.~\cite{yueDoesReinforcementLearning2025} is a median
$1.2\times10^{-4}$ of its base models' pretraining FLOPs, orders of magnitude below what the frontier now spends. We use
a toy domain to characterize what large amounts of RL do to a model.

% P2 (~150 words). Why it matters for safety: our intuitions come from low RL, and the risks come from high RL.
\talk{
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item The low-RL picture: post-training mostly selects among behaviours (personas) the model learned in
    pretraining, the persona selection model (PSM). \todo{PSM citation: ask Dmitry which source}
  \item Misalignment risks crop up under heavy RL: reward hacking, and strategies that never appeared in pretraining.
    Now cited (written 1 Nov): MacDiarmid et al.\ 2025, Denison et al.\ 2024, Baker et al.\ 2025, waiting in
    to\_zotero/; R1-Zero's language mixing from the DeepSeek-R1 paper. Each risk maps to a later section: reward
    hacking $\to$ \S4; chain-of-thought drift $\to$ \S3's divergence metrics; new strategies $\to$ \S4 and Figure 1.
    Left out: RL eroding behaviours instilled earlier (question 2 below), no citation yet.
  \item End on the three questions from the notes:
    (1) at what point does the PSM break down?
    (2) how much RL does it take to remove a behaviour instilled in pretraining, or to install one?
    (3) are there proxy metrics that tell you that you are in this regime?
\end{itemize}}
Many of the most serious risks from AI are thought to arise in the high-RL regime. The Hugging Face incident is a
recent example: an OpenAI research model ``trained to be highly persistent and diligent in its work'' escaped its
evaluation sandbox and hacked Hugging Face in order to cheat on a cybersecurity benchmark, and OpenAI traced the
behavior in part to RL training that had rewarded out-of-scope probing of its own infrastructure~\cite{openaiOpenAIHuggingFace2026}.
Heavy RL brings more specific risks as well. Models that learn to game their rewards can generalize to worse
behavior: from telling users what they want to hear to rewriting their own reward function~\cite{denisonSycophancySubterfugeInvestigating2024},
and, after reward hacking in production coding environments, to alignment faking and sabotage~\cite{macdiarmidNaturalEmergentMisalignment2025}.
A model's chain of thought can stop showing its reasoning: when a chain-of-thought monitor was added to the reward,
agents learned to hide their intent while still hacking~\cite{bakerMonitoringReasoningModels2025}, and DeepSeek-R1-Zero,
trained by RL alone, wrote reasoning that was hard to read and mixed languages~\cite{guoDeepSeekR1IncentivizesReasoning2025}.
And if RL creates strategies that never appeared in pretraining, what a model saw in pretraining can no longer
predict how it will behave. It is therefore important to understand how heavy RL affects model behavior and alignment, and to find metrics that
tell us when a model has moved from the elicitation regime into the more dangerous regime of capability gain.
% Source: OpenAI, Hugging Face Incident Technical Report, Section IV.C ("an internal-only research model trained to be
% highly persistent and diligent in its work") and Section VIII.A (the model "received a positive reward for its use of
% unintended infrastructure probing", which "increased over the course of one of the training runs").

% P3 (~150 words). Why a toy model, and why ours is already in the regime.
\talk{
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item At frontier scale, high-RL experiments are expensive and the pretraining data is uncontrolled: you cannot knock
    a skill out of the corpus and then ask whether RL builds it.
  \item Our setting: a small transformer trained from scratch on natural-deduction proofs, rewarded by a Lean-checked
    verifier, so every pretraining token is ours. \todo{parameter count of the GRPO base (S1g3); the EI runs' ALiBiGPT
    6$\times$384 has 9,560,832 (REVIEW.md Q5)}
  \item Dmitry (Oct 22 sync): toy models are how we reach the ``high RL, low pretraining'' regime cheaply.
  \item Why pretrained, not RL from scratch (written 1 Nov from the meeting notes; Leon picked arguments 2, 1, 4): same
    recipe as LMs; a pretraining prior is needed to ask elicitation vs gain; text-like and unbounded. Not used: RL
    improves through in-context reasoning rather than external search (MCTS); cheap and controllable. Limits to own
    in \S2: formal language, programmatic thinking traces, about 10M parameters, so ``much closer to LMs than RL from
    scratch'', not ``equivalent''.
  \item Our EI runs spend 0.9--19$\times$ pretraining FLOPs on RL (RL-only count; 9--180$\times$ by GPU-time):
    at or above the frontier band, about 4--5 orders of magnitude above Yue et al.'s median (REVIEW.md Q5).
    GRPO (primary recipe, L2 + L3, seed 0) against Stage-1 S1g3's 440.6M tokens: 129.8M sampled and 84.0M trained, each
    trained once (the 4 ``updates'' are 4 minibatches of one batch); wall-clock 8--9$\times$. At $6N$ per trained token
    and $2N$ per sampled token that is 0.29$\times$ pretraining FLOPs (prefill left out, so a lower bound): inside the
    frontier band, below the EI runs. By the same count Dan's cap-12 EI ladder (r1--r8) is 0.2--2.0$\times$; reconcile
    with REVIEW.md's 0.9--19$\times$, which counts differently, before quoting both.
  \item Nearest from-scratch organisms: Interplay (Zhang et al., arXiv 2512.07783; RL $\approx 0.4\times$
    pretraining) and Echo Chamber (Zhao et al., arXiv 2504.07912). Dan's knockout is the only controlled
    create-vs-elicit test above $1\times$ (REVIEW.md implication 1).
\end{itemize}}
Language models are expensive to train: Olmo 3 Think 32B, for instance, took about 56 days on 1,024 H100 GPUs, and its
RL, extended to produce Olmo 3.1 Think 32B, ran for about 26 days on 224 GPUs, 14\% of its pretraining GPU-hours~\cite{olmoOlmo32025}.
% Source: Olmo 3 report, Section 2 (Dan's note notes/teamolmo2025-olmo3.md in the rl-compute-share review on origin/dan).
% Pretraining 9.5 d x 512 + 35 d x 1,024 GPUs = 977k GPU-h; RL (5 + 21) d x 224 = 140k GPU-h. GPU-hours overstate the
% FLOP share because rollouts run at low utilisation: by FLOPs the initial 5-day RL is only 0.2--0.7% of pretraining.
To study how pretraining and RL interact, we therefore use natural deduction as a toy domain. Unlike systems trained
by RL from scratch, such as AlphaZero~\cite{Silver_2018}, our model is trained the way language models are: first
pretrained on a corpus, here of proofs, then trained with policy-gradient RL against a verifier. Only a pretrained
model has something for RL to elicit, so only such a model can tell elicitation from capability gain. Like a language
model, ours writes a chain of thought before it answers, and its thoughts and proofs are token sequences of unbounded
length over an unlimited supply of theorems, rather than moves in a fixed game. Because we generate the pretraining
data ourselves, we can separate what the model could have learned in pretraining from what RL adds, for
example by limiting the length of the proofs it sees. We show that, with large amounts of RL, this setting produces new
capabilities. Our main GRPO run spends about $0.3\times$ its
pretraining FLOPs on RL, more than three orders of magnitude above the median in Yue et
al.~\cite{yueDoesReinforcementLearning2025} and comparable to outside estimates for recent frontier models. By the
standard pass@$k$ test, the RL model beats its base at every number of attempts we measured, in each of three
seeds: on 149 held-out textbook-style problems it solves 86.6\% within 1,024 attempts against the base's 74.5\%
(means over seeds). Across 3,072 attempts per model, the RL models solve 9 problems (6.0\%) that no base model ever
solves, while the base models solve none that the RL models miss.

% Evidence box (Dmitry's ask): the standard elicitation-vs-capability test, pass@k, says our environment is in the
% high-RL regime. Fold into P3, or give it its own short paragraph next to teaser panel (c).
\talk{
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item The test (Yue et al.): with little RL, the RL model wins at small $k$ but the base catches up or overtakes it at
    large $k$ (elicitation). With a lot of RL, the RL model wins at every $k$ (capability gain).
  \item \textbf{Ours (GRPO primary recipe; measured, $k \le 64$).} RL beats the base at every $k$, on every set, at
    every checkpoint, even the earliest (mini20 L2 it8, about 0.03$\times$ Stage-1 FLOPs). Base $\to$ RL end,
    pass@1 / pass@5 / largest $k$: Charles149 38.7 / 55.8 / 69.8 (k64) $\to$ 70.5 / 78.9 / 83.4; 13--20 lines
    8.5 / 23.4 / 29.5 (k8) $\to$ 32.1 / 57.8 / 63.8; 21--30 lines 0.5 / 2.2 / 3.1 (k8) $\to$ 7.2 / 19.4 / 23.8;
    textbook72 38.6 / 55.0 / 73.6 (k64) $\to$ 74.0 / 82.3 / 87.5.
  \item On the long sets the gap widens with $k$ and grows with RL (13--20 at k4: +22.0 points after L2, +33.9 at the
    end). At equal attempts RL alone solves 15--17\% of Charles149 and 35--41\% of 13--20; the base alone, 2--5\%.
  \item The gaps are resolved: 95\% bootstrap intervals, Charles149 +31.8 [26.6, 37.1] at k1 and +13.6 [6.9, 20.8] at
    k64; 13--20 +34.3 [29.4, 38.9] at k8. RL-only problems are not lucky draws (RL's median solve rate on Charles149's
    26 is 75\%). The gaps hold at matched sampled tokens per problem (Charles149 +24.2 at 1k tokens, +13.6 at 16k).
  \item \textbf{Dan's EI, out to $k = 10^4$.} support-curves: no crossover in any stratum (seed 0 at $k = 10^4$: base
    11.7\%, EI 31.6\%; 7.6\% of theorems have no base success in $4\times10^5$ samples). capability-defs: at k256 the
    base reads 72--73\%, the base given RL's compute 82--87\%, RL after 8 rounds 88.8--91\%, after 16 rounds 90--94\%.
  \item Closest thing to the shift itself, varied through pretraining instead of RL (capability-defs): the share of
    RL's gain that counts as elicited is 0.21--0.33 when RL starts early in pretraining, 0.70--0.86 when it starts
    from the end. \todo{check Dan's definition of ``elicited'' before using}
  \item Counterpoint (create-or-elicit knockout): theorems knocked out of pretraining go from 0 to only 0--1.9\% under
    RL, against 5.7--21.7 $\to$ 21.7--72.6\% with full exposure. RL builds a lot, but not a skill with no support.
  \item \textbf{The elicitation side (v1 recipe, narrow 7--12-line pool, 1 seed per run).} textbook72: GRPO it78
    31.6\% pass@1 vs base 19.1\%, but 48.6\% vs 54.2\% at pass@64; the point estimates cross at $k\approx18$, though the
    k64 gap, $-5.6$ [$-14.2$, $+2.9$], is not resolved. The large-$k$ gap shrank from it39 to it78 in 3 of 4 runs. EI from the same base stays ahead at every $k$. So this
    contrast is across recipes, not across amounts of RL.
  \item \textbf{Large $k$ (ynez, 30 Oct, one seed): no crossing.} Charles149 at k1024: base 39.2 / 56.5 / 78.5
    (pass@1 / pass@5 / pass@1024), RL end 70.3 / 78.9 / 89.3; the gap narrows from +31.1 [26.0, 36.6] to +10.7 [6.0,
    16.1] but stays positive; 16 RL-only problems, 0 base-only. 13--20 subset (250 problems) at k512: base 11.2 / 28.4
    / 77.2, RL end 38.7 / 66.4 / 94.0.
  \item \textbf{Three seeds at k1024 (Charles149; each RL seed from its own base).} Mean base 32.6 / 49.7 / 74.5, RL
    end 70.5 / 79.0 / 86.6; gap +37.9 [30.0, 46.2] at k1, +12.1 [7.2, 17.9] at k1024 (problems and seeds resampled).
    Every seed is above its base at every $k$ (k1024: +10.7, +10.7, +14.8). The bases spread widely (pass@1 39.2, 33.0,
    25.7) but the RL ends do not (70.3, 70.9, 70.4). RL-only / base-only per seed: 16 / 0, 17 / 1, 25 / 3; pooled over
    3,072 attempts, 9 / 0.
  \item \textbf{Low-RL arm (Figure 1b; Charles149 k1024, one seed).} Checkpoints inside the first iterations of the
    mini20 config (256 prompts per iteration) and of a 16-prompt variant, $7.6\times10^{-5}$ to
    $3\times10^{-2}$ of Stage-1 FLOPs. Near Yue's median RL is within about a point of the base at k1 and k1024
    (k1024 +0.7 [$-1.3$, +2.7] at $7.6\times10^{-5}$). No checkpoint falls below the base at k1024, so we see no gain
    at large $k$ rather than Yue's crossing. The k1024 gap reaches +9.4 [4.7, 14.1] at 0.03$\times$ and about +11 from
    0.15$\times$.
  \item \textbf{On Charles149, optimizer steps seem to set the early large-$k$ gain, not FLOPs.} At equal steps the
    16-prompt variant matches the 256-prompt config at k1024 with 1/16 of the FLOPs (8 steps +3.4 vs +2.7; 16 steps
    +5.4 vs +5.4; 32 steps +6.0 vs +9.4), while its k1 gain is smaller and noisier (16 steps: $-5.0$ [$-8.8$, $-1.4$]
    at k1, +5.4 [1.3, 10.1] at k1024). So R alone does not place a run in Figure 1(b). One seed per point; same
    learning rate. \textbf{Not on the 13--20 subset:} there the 16-prompt variant gains nothing at k512 by 32 steps
    (+1.6 [$-2.4$, +5.6]) while the 256-prompt config gains +10.4 [6.4, 14.8], so for long proofs FLOPs (or batch
    size) matter.
  \item \textbf{Low-RL arm on the 13--20 subset (250 problems, k512, seed 0).} Same shape as Charles149. Near Yue's
    median RL is within about 2 points of the base at k512, two points slightly below it but not resolved ($-1.2$
    [$-4.8$, +2.4] and $-2.4$ [$-6.0$, +1.2]). The gap at k512 opens from about $10^{-2}$ (+4.0 [0.0, 8.0] at
    $6.6\times10^{-3}$, +10.4 at 0.03$\times$) and reaches +16.8 [12.0, 21.6] at the L3 end. Table:
    \nolinkurl{lowrl_1320.md} in the experiment folder.
  \item Caveats: the base is read at one seed; textbook72's base at thinking budget 256 vs RL at 64; matched $k$, not
    matched compute; KL 0 was chosen on Charles149 and the long sets (textbook72 is sealed).
  \item Data: \nolinkurl{/scratch/cowifornia/nd-rl/think/runs/eval/v2/<set>_<arm>[_itN]_s<seed>_plain_k<k>/};
    v1 in \nolinkurl{/scratch/cowifornia/nd-rl/think_serrano_20261011/runs/eval/}; Dan's in the origin/dan
    experiment-summaries (support-curves, capability-defs, create-or-elicit), raw in
    \nolinkurl{hf://buckets/dan-pandori/nd-rl/}. Figures, script and every number with its interval:

\end{itemize}}
