\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reinforcement learning against a verifier is now the standard way to improve reasoning models. Whether it teaches them
anything new is contested. \citet{yue2025doesrl} find that base models overtake their RL-trained descendants in
pass@$k$ as $k$ grows. \citet{karan2025power} approach RL's gain on a mathematics benchmark by sampling the base from a
sharpened distribution, with no training. Other work reports RL solving problems the base never solved
\citep{abdulsalam2026rewrite,liu2025prorl}. Part of the disagreement is about budgets. A base whose per-attempt success
probability on a problem is $10^{-6}$ will almost never solve it in 1,024 attempts, yet the capability is present in a
sense that matters for evaluation and safety. A capability is defined only relative to a sampling budget
\citep{li2026elicitcreate,brown2024monkeys}.

A claim that RL \emph{created} a capability therefore needs three things:
\begin{itemize}
\item a budget at which the base is judged unable, tied to what RL spent;
\item controls that receive RL's training without RL's proofs;
\item ideally, a base that never had the capability \citep{jain2023mechanistically,deeb2024unlearning}.
\end{itemize}
Interventions on what the base saw exist, and they disagree. \citet{zhang2025interplay} vary a context's share of
pretraining (0\%, 0.1\%, 1\%) in a 100M model and find no transfer from RL below 1\%. \citet{abdulsalam2026rewrite}
exclude shortcuts from pretraining and see GRPO build them. \citet{tsilivis2025rlntp} set the share of long
demonstrations exactly. A strategy removed at the fine-tuning stage did not re-emerge under GRPO
\citep{krishnamurthy2026select}, while a pattern family removed from the RL data did \citep{park2025countdown}. None of
these measures the removed behaviour's rate under the base beyond about a thousand attempts. We remove a single inference composition and its variants from pretraining
and refill the data to the same size. Both of the composition's rules stay in the data, though negation introduction becomes 2.9 times
rarer and double-negation elimination 4.2 times. We then measure the base's rate for the composition at about a million attempts.

We do this in a model organism small enough to allow it. It is a 9.56M-parameter transformer prover for propositional
logic, pretrained from scratch on 155,000 synthetic natural-deduction proofs. It acts through a proof-state interface,
one inference step at a time, and Lean~4 alone decides whether a proof counts. The composition we remove is the
classical reductio \sig{}: to prove a goal $G$, assume $\neg G$, derive a contradiction, conclude $\neg\neg G$
by negation introduction, and eliminate the double negation (\texttt{DN}, Lean's \texttt{Classical.byContradiction}).
Its variants, which apply \texttt{DN} to other derived lines, are removed with it (together \sigp{}, 16,961 of the
155,000 records).
We track six classical theorem families, such as excluded middle and Peirce's law, whose \emph{strict} members can only
be proved by eliminating a double negation the proof itself built. Their 105 held-out strict members, plus one generated
theorem, are never trained on. We then run expert iteration (EI) from each base for 16 rounds: sample, keep what Lean accepts, fine-tune
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}).

\paragraph{Two objections, and the null model.} The first objection is that a model cannot learn what it never saw.
But the knockout bases saw both rules, and five of six still proved held-out strict theorems with \sig{} at large sample
sizes. The second objection is stronger. At the bases' measured rates, EI's 3,936 attempts per round on the 123 strict
training theorems should find a \sig{} proof only rarely: at most about 0.02 to 0.03 per round. We take this waiting-time
arithmetic as the null model and ask, post hoc, what happened beyond it.

\paragraph{Results.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No knockout seed acquired a classical family in 16 rounds} (0/6, which bounds the per-seed rate below
0.39), against 6/6 full-exposure seeds. That pre-registered contrast is largely decided before RL, because one family is
already acquired at the end of pretraining on 5/6 full seeds. The RL-phase contrasts were not pre-registered. During the
RL phase, which includes replay of pretraining data, full seeds gained 8--69 strict held-out theorems and knockout seeds
0--2 (exact Mann--Whitney $p=0.0011$; the same in guided evaluations). On the three full seeds that shared the
knockout's hardware, $p=0.012$. Five of six full seeds against none acquired a new family ($p=0.0032$).
\item \textbf{One percent is enough.} Restoring \sig{} in 1\% of pretraining records, about a tenth of the full set's
share, restored acquisition on 3/3 seeds (exploratory).
\item \textbf{Rare, not absent, mostly in one family.} The knockout bases' successes at large $k$ are almost all on
converse contraposition, the family the full bases had before RL. On the other five families they wrote one \sig{}
proof in 6.7 million attempts. With guided decoding, four of six knockout bases proved a strict theorem in 256
attempts, including seed 0, the only base whose rate is certified out of reach under plain sampling at $T=0.8$ on
the pre-registered pool of 106 theorems (at $T=1$, plain sampling solved \texttt{la\_transfer\_916} on it).
\item \textbf{Beyond the null} (post hoc). The knockout ladders wrote \sig{} from round 1 on theorems that supply the
double negation. Three of six first proved a strict training theorem sooner than their base rates predict: 0.55--0.74 seeds
expected by round 16 against 3 observed ($p = 0.006$--$0.020$ under the null). None carried the step to a family. The
ladder that went furthest, continued to 24 rounds, proved the same 5 of the 106 held-out theorems at rounds 20 and 24,
each more often than at round 16 (the experiment's per-member counts). Its base had no success on three of the five in 18,432 fresh attempts each, though two of those three had one success
each in an earlier, early-stopped sample.
\item \textbf{Measurement.} In three earlier full-exposure runs (seeds s0--s2 of the same prover, after 8 rounds,
on 322 evaluation theorems), 51--60 theorems per run look RL-created at equal sample counts. Between 14 and 34
remain when the base gets RL's compute as attempts, depending on how that compute is counted; 14--21 remain under all
five budget allocations we tried.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A composition knockout in pretraining} (\S\ref{sec:results}), with a dose arm and diagnostics that
separate ``absent'' from ``rare'': base rates at about $10^6$ attempts, certificates, and likelihoods of known proofs
along twelve of the fifteen ladders. The primary indicator, the certificates and the likelihood expectations were pre-registered, the
dose arm by a dated amendment. The certificate expectation, at least five of six knockout bases certified, was
missed: one of six on the pre-registered 106 strict theorems, two of six on the 105 family members. The RL-phase contrasts and the waiting-time analysis are post hoc. An analyst who had not
written the experiment's code recounted it independently.
\item \textbf{A compute-matched measurement protocol} (\S\ref{sec:setup}, \S\ref{sec:case}), applied to three earlier
full-exposure runs, and at one allocation to the knockout. It gives the base a budget of attempts matched to RL's
compute under several defensible allocations, so that the ``created'' set becomes a curve in the budget. It brackets
each theorem's success rate between sampling bounds and a likelihood estimate, certifies unreachability, and nets out
RL-free training. It builds on budget-relative definitions \citep{li2026elicitcreate,davidson2023retraining} and on
base-to-RL exchange rates \citep{sun2026budgeted}.
\item \textbf{An open organism}: a from-scratch prover with a sound judge, likelihoods of arbitrary proofs at every
checkpoint, and pretraining that can be edited one composition at a time. Code, data, checkpoints and per-arm compute
are public.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What transfers.} The numbers do not: the 1\% dose, the $10^{-6}$ rates and the dynamics of EI belong to
this organism. Three findings may.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The waiting time scales as $1/(kNr)$.} Here $k$ is attempts per problem, $N$ the number of training
problems that need the composition, a variant or the lemma route of Table~\ref{tab:concepts} (here 123 of 4,495), and $r$ the base's rate. The null is a property of the RL pool
as much as of pretraining.
\item \textbf{Cued practice did not yield a family within 16 rounds.} Each knockout ladder wrote thousands of
accepted proofs using \sig{} or a variant where the problem supplied the double negation, and almost none where it did
not.
\item \textbf{Exposure set what this EI found within 16 rounds, not what the model could learn.} In this organism,
16 demonstrations taught excluded middle to a base pretrained with no \texttt{DN} at all, even with \texttt{DN}-free
replay.
\end{itemize}
Failure at modest $k$ does not show absence \citep{brown2024monkeys,hofstatter2025elicitation}. Our knockout bases prove
none of the 106 held-out strict theorems at $k=256$ under plain sampling in the pre-registered evaluation (one proves
one in a second draw), yet guided decoding or a million plain attempts reveals the composition.
