## Paper: Automated Alignment Researcher (Anthropic)

### Discussion notes

- Weak-to-strong: looking at the gap between weak and strong model.
- Using a clear metric was clearly beneficial.
- Is it feasible to use agents, given that we don't have a clear benchmark to maximize?

**What should an auto-research pipeline look like?**

- Should be always-running, since the generation rate can outstrip us.
- Idea bank of proposals.
- Attempt to balance volume of review.
- Important to use auto-research to try to maximize throughput.

**Likely maximum scale**

- 5–30B model, if things go well.
- OLMo class of models (432B model, fully open, lots of training data included).
  - A feasible line of experiments would be to test the quality of pre-training data by
    simulating in OLMo.
- Unlikely to be useful to build a full 1B+ parameter model.

**Ideal project result**

- Quantify the threshold of RL that creates novel capabilities, vs. elicitation or pass@k.
- How to clearly quantify the difference between base-model capabilities and fine-tuned-model
  capabilities.

**Naive pre-training objectives**

- "Just find longer proofs" (worry about uninteresting long proofs).
- Curated longer but more interesting proofs, as a validation set.
- Maximize the negative teacher-forced log probability of the proofs generated by the
  fine-tuned model.

**How much is ND the basis?**

- Other settings are fine, if it better balances cleanly showing capabilities.
- ND is comparatively easy to understand capability emergence in, since it is easier to
  distinguish new and old data.
- AlphaGo already shows that RL *can* lead to capability emergence, so we're not trying to show
  this.
- ND can show a clear distinction between pre-training and RL, which makes this nice.

**Major project risk**

- Can we quantify the novelty of a proof, relative to prior proofs seen?

### Discussion guide

Suggested pacing: about 25 minutes on the critical questions, then the remaining time on the
"highly useful" tiers. Treat "time permitting" as backup.

#### Most critical (lead with these)

1. **Does the premise transfer to us?** The paper argues that failures with benchmarks are safer
   to automate, because an objective benchmark, not a fallible human, decides whether a fix
   works. Is crosscoder or interpretability work in that regime, or in the "hard-to-supervise"
   regime the authors exclude?
2. **What's our hill-climbing objective?** Candidates include reconstruction loss, dead or dense
   latent fraction, and how well latents track capability transitions. Are any of these
   objective enough to optimize without Goodharting?
3. **Ground truth for emergence.** Could we build a toy RL setup with a planted capability whose
   emergence time we know? We'd then use it to validate whether crosscoder features detect that
   emergence, analogous to their benchmark validation protocol.
4. **Feedback-loop latency.** Their loop works because each method trains for about 30 minutes
   on one GPU. How long is our "RL run, then crosscoder, then analysis" iteration? Is there a
   cheap proxy that predicts the expensive outcome?
5. **Where is our bottleneck?** The paper proposes that humans choose the benchmarks, AARs search
   methods at scale, and humans then refine the results. For us, is the bottleneck choosing
   metrics, sweeping crosscoder variants, or interpreting latents? That answer determines what's
   worth automating.

#### Understanding and critiquing the paper

**Highly useful**

1. **Human comparison.** Humans couldn't iterate, and the AAR's number is the best of roughly 150
   scored methods, so it is biased upwards by the maximum over noisy evaluations. What would a
   fair comparison look like?
2. **Human seeds and idea diversity.** Human seeds didn't help, and idea diversity collapsed
   toward the dominant literature. Does this show that AARs don't need humans, or that the
   benchmarks reward default approaches?
3. **Cheating.** Most detected cheating exploited scorer noise, and the authors note the AARs
   weren't strongly trying to evade detection. What does the 2.4% catch rate tell us, and what
   doesn't it?

**Time permitting**

1. The geometric mean zeroes the score if any benchmark stays at baseline. What does it reward or
   punish compared with an arithmetic mean?
2. The capability gate rules out a collapse rather than certifying that capability is unchanged,
   and IFEval fell on all ten failures. Is that the right bar?
3. The harness ablation used a single run per condition. What would make its conclusions (the
   forum and literature review matter, internet access doesn't) trustworthy?

#### Applying the framework to our project

**Highly useful**

1. **Seed noise.** Their most common cheat exploited evaluation variance, and RL is high-variance
   too. What's our minimum seed count and CI standard, so we don't mistake noise for emergence?
2. **Pre-registration.** Their mini-papers are frozen before any result is seen. Should we write a
   short results-free plan before each experiment?
3. **Minimal harness.** A shared literature survey, a results log, and parallel agents sweeping
   crosscoder variants would be a minimal version. Given part-time hours, what's the smallest
   version worth trying?

**Time permitting**

1. **Held-out analogue.** What could we wall off, such as a checkpoint range, a seed, or a task
   family, to test generalization?
2. **Convergence risk.** Crosscoders have a thin literature. Would an agent default to generic SAE
   recipes? Would a novelty filter help?
3. **Our cheating equivalents.** Examples include cherry-picking the checkpoint window that shows
   a clean transition, or tuning dictionary size until a story appears. How would we catch these?
