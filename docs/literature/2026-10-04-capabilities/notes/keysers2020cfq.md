# Measuring compositional generalisation (CFQ): compound divergence at fixed atom divergence

Paper: [@keysers2020cfq] (Keysers, Schärli, Scales, Buisman, Furrer, Kashubin, Momchev, Sinopalnikov,
Stafiniak, Tihon, Tsarkov, Wang, van Zee, Bousquet, ICLR 2020)
Source: arXiv 1912.09713v2 (HTML rendering read: abstract, Sec. 1-2, Sec. 4 opening, Sec. 5, Sec. 7;
Sec. 3 dataset construction and appendices skimmed only).

## Learnings

- **Definition.** Compositional generalisation is "the ability to systematically generalize to composed
  test examples of a certain distribution after being exposed to the necessary components during training
  on a different distribution" (Sec. 1).
- **Atoms and compounds.** For data generated by rules, "the atoms are the individual rules, while the
  compounds are the subgraphs of the directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) that correspond to the rule
  applications" (Sec. 2).
- **DBCA principles** (Sec. 2.1): "Similar atom distribution: All atoms present in the test set are also
  present in the train set" (and distributed alike); "Different compound distribution: The distribution of
  compounds in the train set is as different as possible from the distribution in the test set".
- **Formalisation** (Sec. 2.1): Chernoff coefficient C_α(P‖Q) = Σ_k p_k^α q_k^{1−α};
  D_A(V‖W) = 1 − C_0.5(F_A(V)‖F_A(W)) (Bhattacharyya) and D_C(V‖W) = 1 − C_0.1(F_C(V)‖F_C(W)), where α = 0.1
  "reflects the intuition that it is more important whether a certain compound occurs in P (train) than
  whether the probabilities in P (train) and Q (test) match exactly". Compounds are weighted "to avoid
  double-counting compounds that are highly correlated with some of their super-compounds". Preferred
  benchmark: maximum compound divergence with D_A ≤ 0.02; splits built by a greedy algorithm (Sec. 4).
- **Results.** All models > 95 % on random splits, but "The mean accuracy on the MCD splits is below 20% for
  all architectures" (Sec. 5.2; Table 4: CFQ MCD 14.9 / 17.9 / 18.9 % for LSTM+attention / Transformer /
  Universal Transformer; SCAN MCD 6.1 / 1.1 / 1.2 %). "the baseline models are able to capture the
  superficial structure of the dataset, but fail to capture the compositional structure". Compound
  divergence explains accuracy across splits far better than length: R² "between 0.11 and 0.22 for the
  input and output length ratios and between 0.81 and 0.88 for the compound divergence" (Sec. 5.2).
- **Protocol hygiene.** "hyperparameter tuning should be done on a random split" so that the test
  distribution does not leak into the model through tuning (Sec. 5.1).

## Evidence and limitations

- Two datasets (CFQ: 443 rules; SCAN: 38 rules, "only 38 in comparison to 443", Sec. 5.3), three
  architectures, 5 replicates with 95 % CIs. The divergence-accuracy relation is shown on splits the method
  itself constructs (Fig. 2). Models are trained from scratch; there is no pre-train / fine-tune contrast.
- The compound inventory is a design choice (a large subset of subgraphs, weighted, keep the top ones);
  results could depend on it. Appendix details (L.4) were not read.

## Connections and questions

- **Definition offered:** compositional generalisation = accuracy on a test distribution whose *compounds*
  (rule-application subgraphs) diverge from training while its *atoms* (rules) do not; the dose variable is
  D_C (a Chernoff divergence with α = 0.1, which mostly counts whether a compound was ever seen).
- **New vs better access:** not addressed, but the measure gives an item-level novelty axis independent
  of any model: a theorem (or a proof) is compositionally novel relative to the *pretraining corpus* if its
  proof DAG contains compounds that the corpus lacks while its atoms (inference rules) are all present. Our
  rule: RL's gains count as *new composition* when they concentrate on items with high compound
  divergence from the pretraining corpus, and as *re-weighting of seen compounds* when they sit at low
  divergence. Equivalently, compare the accuracy-vs-D_C slope of pend and r16: a flatter slope after RL
  (and not after replay-only training) is the signature of compositional generalisation acquired in RL.
- **Null / floor:** not handled (greedy exact match). Divergence is a property of the data, so it
  supplies a model-free "how far from training" axis, which is what pass@k lacks; budgets still have to be
  added when the accuracy is a sampled solve rate.
- **Transfer to our setting:** atoms = ND inference rules / Lean tactics-terms (`Or.inl`, `Or.elim`,
  `Classical.byContradiction`, `absurd`, …); compounds = subtrees of the proof term up to size 3-4. Count
  F_C over the pretraining corpus (155,000 theorems) once, then score every reference proof and every
  accepted RL proof. The excluded-middle proof (every move in pretraining, no premise-free A ∨ ¬A theorem)
  is the textbook case of low atom / high compound divergence; the question is whether its *subtrees* (not
  just its moves) appear in pretraining, which this measure answers. Cost: CPU parsing of the corpus, no
  GPU. Failure modes: the compound vocabulary must be fixed before looking at RL proofs (pre-register size
  and weighting); a proof the RL model finds may use different compounds from the reference proof, so
  score both; replay data in EI is part of "training", so divergence must be computed against pretraining
  plus replay, and separately against the RL-accepted training targets.
- Related: `hupkes2020compositionality.md` (the systematicity split this generalises), `okawa2023multiplicative.md`
  and `arora2023skills.md` (why novel compounds are hard), `yu2023skillmix.md` (combinatorial novelty as an
  anti-memorisation argument).
