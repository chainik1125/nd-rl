# Why the hardest textbook problems are hard, and which small pieces they need

Generated by `code/pretrain_data/hard_problems.py` from `per_problem.csv` (401 evaluated problems; scraped sets
v0, v1, batch3, hand14, lean_v2 plus derived), the release's reference proofs, and Dan's K12 pool (155,000 rows).
Hard = never solved by either RL seed (64 samples each) or reference proof ≥ 14 lines: 155 problems, 136 distinct renaming classes (hand14 repeats problems of batch3 and v1; twins are counted once below). The sealed 72 are not read.

## 0. Summary

**Why the reference proofs are long.** Four things, usually two of them together, in the 77 distinct hard
scraped problems (67 never solved by either RL seed; 52 with reference ≥ 14 lines):

1. **Biconditionals.** 42 of 77 contain a transcribed ↔, i.e. (a → b) ∧ (b → a). Every ↔ in the goal costs two →I boxes
   and an ∧I; every ↔ among the premises costs an ∧E before each →E. Nested ↔ multiplies: Pelletier 12, a ↔ between two
   ↔s, is 102 lines with 18 →I boxes at depth 4. Roy E6.20d (71 lines) refutes three negated ↔s by building each one
   inside nested ¬I boxes. 38 of 77 goals are conjunctions, mostly for this reason. The mean reference of the ≥ 14-line
   group is 27.8 lines, 2.7 boxes deep, 7 boxes.
2. **Boxes, not steps.** Only 21 % of the hard proofs' lines are at depth 0; 34 of 77 need depth ≥ 3, which the generator
   never produces (K12 depth ≤ 2). Conditional goals with no premises (fm2024 022: depth 5, 13 lines) are pure box nesting.
3. **Classical detours.** 31 of 77 contain a reductio (assume ¬goal, derive ⊥, ¬I, DN), and De Morgan-type goals need a
   reductio around nested ¬I boxes: ¬(P ∧ Q) → (¬P ∨ ¬Q) is 15 lines at depth 4 for one ∧I and three ¬E.
4. **Case splits that re-prove the goal per case.** A three-way disjunction premise (calgary 4) costs 18 lines, 14 of them
   inside ∨E boxes; nested ∨E (ORE citing ORE) is 12× rarer in K12 than in these proofs.

**Length is not the only reason they fail.** 25 of the 67 never-solved scraped problems have references of 13 lines or
fewer, inside the RL targets' 7–16 range. Among them: `P, Q, P → Q, Q → R ⊢ R` (5 lines, 4 premises; K12 has at most 3
premises), `P, Q, R, S ⊢ (P ∧ Q) ∧ (R ∧ S)` (7 lines, 4 premises), `P → Q ⊢ (R → P) → (R → Q)` (7 lines, two nested →I
boxes), and a 6-line nested-∧E problem. Six of the 67 have ≥ 4 premises.

**Do the hard proofs need rules K12 lacks?** Mostly no, at the level of single pieces: of the 361 distinct 2–6-line
sub-theorems inside the hard proofs, 85 % occur in K12 at least in skeleton form (same rule pattern, smaller formulas),
and 36 of the 67 never-solved scraped problems are made entirely of such known pieces. Not a single hard proof's
whole-proof skeleton is in K12. What K12 lacks is the **joins between pieces**, visible in the rule bigrams (§ 6.1):

| rule combination (citing ← cited) | hard proofs, per 1,000 citations | K12, per 1,000 | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| →E ← ∧E (use a conjunct as a conditional: ↔-elimination) | 50 | 0.55 | ≈ 90× |
| ¬E ← ¬I (a derived negation closes a contradiction) | 9.8 | 0.08 | 128× |
| ∧E ← ∧E (nested conjunction unpacking) | 8 | 0.26 | ≈ 35× |
| ∨I ← ¬I (De Morgan direction) | 5.5 | 0.16 | 34× |
| →E ← ¬I | 7.3 | 0.29 | 25× |
| ∧I ← →I (↔-introduction) | 55 | 3.0 | 18× |
| ¬E ← →E | 26 | 1.8 | 14× |
| ∨E ← ∨E (nested case split) | 6.4 | 0.51 | 12× |
| →E ← →E (chains) | 21 | 2.6 | 8× |

K12 contains exactly one proof whose skeleton is `((P → Q) ∧ R), P ⊢ Q`, the ∧E-then-→E join, which 13 hard proofs use.
The pieces K12 lacks outright (§ 4) are all ∧-unpacking shapes: `(P ∧ (Q ∧ R)) ⊢ R`, `((P → Q) ∧ R), (P ∧ S) ⊢ Q`,
`(P → (Q → R)), (P → Q), P ⊢ R`, `((P → ¬Q) ∧ R), P, Q ⊢ ⊥`.

**What this says for the pretrain.** Charles's hypothesis holds in a specific form: the small problems to add are not
"the hard rules" one at a time (K12 has every rule) but 4–8-line problems built from the joins above, plus the three
structural gaps that no small problem fixes: ≥ 4 premises, depth ≥ 3, and ↔ as a formula shape. The derived set says the
same from the other side: its hard problems are the distractor-scaling (≥ 4 premises), conjoined and negated-conjunction
nuances, whose unknown pieces are again ∧E-then-→E joins.

## 2. What the lines pay for

Each non-premise line is attributed to one of five costs. `case split` = ORI/ORE lines, the assumptions of case boxes and everything inside them; `classical` = reductio boxes (¬G assumed, ¬I, DN) and their contents plus other DN/¬E/⊥E; `conditional` = →I boxes and contents; `∧/∨ shuffle` = ∧E, ∧I and plain ∨I; `→E chain` = →E lines. Inner boxes are charged to the innermost box, so for a goal like A → (B → C) the outer →I box is charged only for what is not inside the inner one.

## 3. How much of each hard proof is made of pieces K12 already contains

A piece is a sub-theorem of 2–6 lines: the theorem proved at a line, with only the premises and open assumptions its sub-proof cites. Two kinds of coverage: **class** = the piece's curry-aware renaming class occurs in K12 as a problem; **skeleton** = after replacing every subformula whose internal structure the sub-proof never uses by an atom, the resulting theorem occurs in K12 (so `((P→Q)∧(Q→P)) ⊢ (P→Q)` has skeleton `(A ∧ B) ⊢ A`). Skeleton coverage is what a model that generalises over formula shape would need.
